How this estimate is actually calculated

Heating and cooling demand are estimated using the degree-day method, a standard simplified building-energy technique: a reference 1,500 sq ft Toronto home with average insulation, heated by a gas furnace, is assumed to use about 2,200 m³ of natural gas a year for heating and 1,800 kWh of electricity for cooling. Every other city, home size, and insulation level scales from that reference in proportion to local heating and cooling degree days (base 18°C), floor area, and an insulation multiplier (drafty homes use about 25% more energy than average; well-insulated homes use about 20% less).

City search covers roughly 40 cities and towns spanning every Canadian province and territory, each with its own approximate heating/cooling degree days. Electricity, gas, and grid-carbon figures are set at the provincial level, since utility rates and grid mix are provincial/utility matters, not city-specific ones. If your exact town isn't listed, picking the nearest city on the list is a reasonable stand-in — this is the same approximation real energy-modeling tools make when they map a location to its nearest reference weather station.

Heating system differences are handled by converting the reference into a "useful heat" energy demand (accounting for a gas furnace's ~92% efficiency), then dividing by whichever system's efficiency applies: gas furnace (~92% AFUE), oil furnace (~85% AFUE), electric heaters (~100% efficient but a much higher primary-energy cost), or a cold-climate heat pump (seasonal COP of ~2.8). Note that natural gas distribution isn't available everywhere in Canada — it's far less common in Atlantic Canada and the territories, where oil and electric heating are more typical — so the gas option is offered everywhere for flexibility, with a placeholder rate where no real municipal network exists.

Savings from temperature setbacks use the U.S. Department of Energy's long-standing rule of thumb: roughly 1% off your heating (or cooling) bill for each degree Fahrenheit you set back for 8 hours, about 1.8% per degree Celsius. This is applied to the setback amount you choose, scaled by how many hours a day it would realistically apply — 8 hours (overnight) if your home isn't empty during the day, or 17 hours (overnight plus a typical workday) if it is. A small additional "automation" bonus (5%, 3%, or 1%) reflects that a smart thermostat catches things people forget, biggest if you're not currently doing any scheduling yourself.

Costs and emissions use approximate, illustrative electricity, natural gas, and heating-oil rates by province (heating oil is priced flat at ~$1.25/L everywhere, since it's typically sold by local distributors rather than a regulated utility), unless you've entered your own rates. Emissions use approximate provincial grid carbon intensities plus standard combustion factors for natural gas (1.88 kg CO2/m³) and heating oil (2.75 kg CO2/L).

Payback period simply divides the thermostat's cost by your total estimated annual savings — it doesn't account for financing, rebates, or installation complexity.
